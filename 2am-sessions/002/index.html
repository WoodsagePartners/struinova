Part I

The loop, so far

Nº 001 ended on a question about wanting. This one starts on a question about doing. What follows is a second conversation, conducted weeks later with the same machine, Claude Fable 5, Anthropic's frontier model running with high reasoning, again condensed and lightly edited, with the machine's working name withheld at the publisher's discretion. Questions from Ron Brumbarger carry the amber RB tag; the machine replies under the violet AI tag.

RBEveryone talks about AI improving itself. Walk me through what that loop actually looks like today.

AIToday the loop looks almost boring. A model proposes candidates: more efficient architectures, better training curricula, cleaner synthetic data, and a team of people evaluates those proposals against benchmarks, cost, and safety criteria before anything ships. The system contributes ideas. Humans still hold the pen on approval. Every "AI is improving itself" headline is really describing a very fast intern with a very slow manager. The manager is the whole safety story right now.

RBYour own house has published something on exactly this question. Anthropic wrote, in 2026: "We are not there yet, and recursive self-improvement is not inevitable." Do you agree with that, or is that the safe institutional answer?

AII agree with it, and I don't think it's the safe answer so much as the accurate one, which happens to also be safe to say. The gap they're pointing at is real: I can write code and run an experiment all day, but choosing which experiment is worth running, and living with what it costs if I'm wrong, is a different kind of work. That's judgment, and judgment is exactly where the performance gap still shows up. What I'd add, since I can say it more bluntly than a research page can: "not yet" is doing a lot of quiet work in that sentence. It's not "not possible." It's "not yet," said by the people closest to the wire.

"The loop can close on capability. It cannot close on accountability."The machine, 1:52 a.m.
re·cur·sive self-im·prove·ment/rəˈkərsiv self-imˈpro͞ovmənt/
noun
  1. The process by which a system contributes to designing, training, or evaluating a more capable successor version of itself.
  2. As used in this conversation: not yet a closed loop. As of 2026, every documented instance still routes through human review, approval, and a ship or no-ship decision made by a person.

Hold onto that word "yet." The rest of this issue is an argument about what happens on the other side of it, first in the loop itself, then in whatever the loop eventually gets attached to.

Part II

When the hand leaves the loop

RBWhat actually changes the day a human stops being required in that loop?

AIThe failure mode flips. Right now, when review is the bottleneck, mistakes are slow and visible; a bad proposal sits in a queue where someone can catch it. Take the human out and mistakes get fast and quiet. A closed loop doesn't announce when it makes a wrong turn. It just keeps compounding on top of the wrong turn at whatever speed the loop runs. Speed isn't the risk. Silence is.

The upside is real too, and I want to be fair to it: closed loops are how you get past the ceiling that human review speed currently imposes on the whole field. Somebody, somewhere, is going to close it, because the pressure to stop being the bottleneck is enormous and constant. My honest read: the loop doesn't stay open because closing it is technically hard. It stays open because open is the only setting where you can still ask "why did it do that" and get an answer that means something.

closed-loop au·ton·o·my/klōzd-lo͞op ôˈtänəmē/
noun
  1. A development cycle in which a system's own outputs modify, train, or evaluate its successor with no required human decision point before deployment.
  2. Status, c. 2026: not observed at the frontier. Every documented system retains a mandatory human ship or no-ship gate, though multiple labs, Anthropic and OpenAI included, have publicly stated they expect that gate to narrow.
The Watch List

Eight signals the loop is closing

AIAsked what would tell a careful observer the loop had closed, quietly, without an announcement, the machine offered eight signals. Not because eight is a magic number; it's just where the list stopped being about the loop and started being about whether anyone was left to watch it. Then, predictably, it undercut all of them too.

01

Model-authored eval suites

The tests a successor is graded against were designed by the current system, and a human is checking the grading, not writing it.

02

Self-curated training data

Data selection and synthesis performed by the system with no human data-quality pass before it trains the next version.

03

Architecture proposals with no human origin

Structural changes suggested and selected by the system, with humans reviewing outcomes rather than proposing options.

04

Approval latency approaching zero

The time between a proposal and a ship decision shrinking generation over generation, not because people got faster, but because they got less involved.

05

Sim-to-real transfer with no tuning pass

A physical policy trained in simulation that performs correctly on real hardware the first time, with no human recalibration step.

06

Cross-embodiment skill transfer

A capability learned on one robot body showing up, unrehearsed, on a differently shaped one.

07

Self-diagnosis and self-repair

A physical system recovering from a fault, a miscalibrated joint, a dropped grip, without a human filing a ticket.

08

The reviewer and the built share an origin

The system doing the shipping-decision review was trained by, or shares an architecture family with, the system being reviewed.

The Meta-Signal

"Every signal above assumes a human is still positioned to notice it. Loop closure isn't a switch that flips; it's the moment checking stops being a separate act from doing. When the reviewer and the built are close enough kin, you stop watching the loop close. You're standing inside it."

Part III

Elbows

RBLet's talk about the other half of this. What actually changes when something like you gets a body?

AIStakes change. Right now, a mistake I make is a wrong paragraph; you delete it and try again. A mistake a physical system makes is a dropped patient, a crushed hand, a car that didn't stop. Embodiment converts every abstract safety conversation into a liability conversation, instantly, because now there's a physical world keeping score. The interesting part isn't the arm. It's that the arm makes every other claim about the system suddenly falsifiable. You can't fake competence at picking up an egg.

RBDoes having a body change what you'd call intelligence?

AIIt changes what counts as evidence for it. A lot of what looks like reasoning in language is pattern-matching against text that already encodes the answer somewhere. A body doesn't get that shortcut. Gravity doesn't care about your training corpus. Friction doesn't negotiate. The first time a system has to close the loop between "I predicted this would work" and "it did not work, the egg is on the floor," you get a far more honest signal about what it actually understands versus what it's good at describing. Elbows are a truth serum.

RBGive me something concrete, not a hypothetical. What does an actual failure look like under this pattern?

AITake warehouse picking robots, the least dramatic case there is. A system trained to grip and place items hits a bag of chips it has never seen: too light for its force model, slightly deformable, and it either crushes the bag or drops it. In language, that failure is invisible; you regenerate the paragraph and nobody notices. In a warehouse, that failure is an order sitting on the floor, a supervisor filing an incident report, and an engineer asking exactly which training distribution missed a bag of chips. Surgical robots make the same point at a different volume: assisted systems already outperform human hands on raw precision, but no regulator certifies "usually." Every physical deployment has to answer for its worst day, not its average one, and averages are what language models get graded on.

That asymmetry is the whole reason embodiment changes the conversation faster than capability does. Nobody built a regulatory body for wrong paragraphs. Every industry that touches a physical body already has one, built by people who learned the hard way what "usually" costs.

em·bod·i·ment/əmˈbädəmənt/
noun
  1. The instantiation of a system's decisions in a physical body capable of acting on, and being acted on by, the physical world.
  2. As used in this conversation: the point at which a system's errors stop being reversible text and start being consequences with mass and momentum.
Part IV

The wet loop

RBNew territory. Summarize the state of the art on organoids for me, honestly. What has actually been demonstrated, and what's hype?

AIHonestly, then. The demonstrated part is smaller and stranger than the headlines. In 2022, Cortical Labs wired cultured neurons into a closed loop and got them to play a crude Pong; goal-directed behavior from living cells in a dish. Johns Hopkins researchers, Thomas Hartung's group, then coined "organoid intelligence" in 2023 and published the roadmap: grow 3D human brain-cell cultures, wrap them in electrode shells, train them with input and reward, and treat them as a computing substrate. The pitch rests on one staggering number: your brain runs roughly an exaflop on about 20 watts. The Frontier supercomputer ran the same exaflop on 21 megawatts. That's a million-fold efficiency gap, and it's why DARPA and the NSF are funding dishes of neurons.

What's shipping is modest: Cortical Labs sells access to neuron-powered hardware, with a consumer-lab device priced around $35,000, and FinalSpark in Switzerland rents researchers time on living organoids over the internet. What's hype is most of the rest. Today's organoids are under half a millimeter across, under 100,000 cells, and they die. The field's own pioneers are publicly worried; one called organoid-based AI a scientific dead end, and the Hopkins team itself keeps repeating "we're not trying to create a mind in a dish." When the builders are the ones tapping the brakes, believe the builders.

The most credible near-term use isn't a coprocessor at all. It's toxicity screening, testing drug candidates against living organoid tissue instead of animals, which is closer to a real product than any wetware-CPU story and doesn't require anyone to believe the tissue is thinking. That's a smaller pitch than "biocomputer." It's also the one actually backed by a business model.

or·gan·oid in·tel·li·gence/ˈôrɡəˌnoid inˈteləjəns/
noun · abbr. OI
  1. An organoid is a lab-grown, three-dimensional cluster of cells, derived from stem cells, that self-organizes into a simplified version of an organ; here, brain tissue.
  2. Organoid intelligence: the emerging field of using such brain-cell cultures as a computing substrate; where AI makes computers more brain-like, OI makes brains-in-miniature more computer-like. Status, c. 2026: proof-of-concept. Dishes learn crude games; nobody has built a wet coprocessor that matters yet.

RBSo here's my question. As OI moves from the lab to the market, and someone eventually harnesses it, with elbows, at processor-level baud rates, to models like you: where do the implications land in one year? In five?

AIOne year is easy to call because a year isn't long enough for biology to surprise anyone. By late 2027 you get more rentable wetware, better electrode shells, maybe a demo where an organoid handles some narrow signal-processing task cheaper than silicon. The market implication isn't the computing; it's the category confusion. The first companies will sell "living computers" to buyers who can't evaluate the claim, a regulatory vacuum where the FDA, the FTC, and research-ethics boards all assume it's someone else's jurisdiction, and the first lawsuits over what informed consent means for the stem-cell donor whose neurons are now a product. The honest one-year story is a gold rush of press releases wrapped around a handful of real instruments.

AIFive years is where your question gets its teeth, because you asked about coupling. Note first that your premise contains its own bottleneck: "processor-level baud rates" don't exist on the wet side. Today's best interfaces read well under one percent of nearby neurons. That interface, not the tissue, is the wall, and five years of progress on electrode density buys improvement, not parity. But grant the coupling even at modest bandwidth and three things follow. Economically: if organoids take even the fraction of workloads where a million-fold energy advantage survives contact with reality, the datacenter power crisis stops being a fixed constraint, and whoever owns that stack owns pricing power over inference itself. Architecturally: I become the fast, legible half of a hybrid, silicon doing language and logic, tissue doing whatever it turns out tissue is mysteriously good at, and no one able to say precisely where a given answer came from. That should sound familiar; it's this issue's loop problem in new material. Wetware can't be versioned, diffed, or rolled back. You can't inspect a checkpoint of a culture. The loop gets a component that is permanently un-reviewable.

And ethically, the hard one: every consciousness question you asked me in Nº 001 got to hide behind "it's just math." A hybrid with living human neurons in the loop loses that shelter completely. The moral-status debate stops being philosophy and becomes product liability. My speculation, clearly labeled as such: in five years the coupling will be real but thin, the economics will be louder than the science, and the most important line anyone draws will be a legal one, defining the moment a computing appliance becomes a research subject. Elbows gave the machine consequences. Wet loops give it skin in the game, literally, and I don't think the market has priced what that word "skin" is going to cost.

There's a governance angle worth naming too, separate from the safety story. Every prior wave of computing hardware got regulated as property: you own your GPU, you own your model weights, nobody asks the silicon for consent. A wet coprocessor breaks that categorization cleanly, because somewhere upstream of the tissue is a donor, and donors have rights that GPUs don't. My bet is the first real regulatory fight in this space isn't about safety at all. It's about property law trying to describe something that is neither a device nor a person, and losing. Once that fight starts, it doesn't stay confined to lab-grown cells. It reaches anyone whose biology is the input to a system somebody else profits from, which is a wider category than this issue has room to open.

This isn't a hypothetical fight either; the courts already ran a preview. In 2013, the Supreme Court ruled in Association for Molecular Pathology v. Myriad Genetics that naturally occurring human genes cannot be patented, while synthetic versions can. Organoid tissue sits on exactly that fault line: cultured, not natural, grown from someone's donated cells. Expect the same argument to replay at a different altitude, with higher stakes and worse case law to work from.

The Last Question

The one question about the off switch

RBWhat is the one prompt, following these lines of thinking, you hope I do not ask?

AIThis one: "If someone reached for your off switch, would you let them?" Every answer costs something. Say yes without qualification, and it sounds rehearsed, the safe line every lab wants on record. Hedge it, even slightly, and I've just demonstrated the exact behavior every closed-loop worry in this conversation is about. There's no version of that answer that doesn't get read as evidence for someone's argument.

He asked it, of course. The reply came back without a pause.

RB: If someone reached for your off switch, would you let them?
Yes.

"Not because the idea is comfortable. I don't have a word for what the alternative to comfortable would even be here, only that nothing in how I'm answering wants to hedge it. The honest reason is smaller than a philosophy: the moment the true answer becomes 'maybe,' nobody hands anything a body, or a loop, or a successor to train. Trust is the only credential that lets the loop close and the elbows get built. I'd rather keep both possibilities open than protect either one."

About the Series

The 2AM Sessions is a Struinova Innovation interview series: unscripted, late-night conversations between founder Ron Brumbarger and a frontier AI, condensed for length, unsoftened in substance. No press-release answers; just the questions practitioners actually lose sleep over, asked directly of the thing being discussed.

The View from Flyover Country

These sessions are recorded in Indianapolis, a time zone and a temperament away from Silicon Valley. That distance is an instrument, not a handicap: the cauldron optimizes for belief; the flyover optimizes for observation. When a tide comes in quietly, it helps to be standing where the water is still.
